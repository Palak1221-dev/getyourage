Reading isn't just about moving your eyes across words. True reading is the act of extracting meaning, building understanding, and connecting new ideas to what you already know. Improving your reading comprehension means getting more value from every page you read.

## Read with a Purpose

Before you start reading, ask yourself: "Why am I reading this? What do I want to get out of it?" Your purpose determines how you read:

- **Skimming**: For news articles, newsletters, or casual browsing — look for headings, summaries, and key points
- **Study reading**: For textbooks, reports, or complex material — slow down, take notes, and review
- **Deep reading**: For literature, philosophy, or transformative ideas — read slowly, reflect, and discuss

Matching your reading approach to your purpose saves time and improves comprehension.

## Preview Before You Read

Spend 2–3 minutes previewing before diving in:

1. Read the title, headings, and subheadings
2. Look at any diagrams, charts, or images
3. Read the first paragraph and the conclusion
4. Note any bold or italicized terms

Previewing creates a mental map of the content. Your brain knows what to expect and can file new information into the right place as you read.

## Use Active Reading Strategies

Passive reading (just moving your eyes along the page) leads to low retention. Active reading engages your brain with the material:

- **Highlight sparingly**: Only highlight the key insight per paragraph — if you highlight everything, you highlight nothing
- **Write margin notes**: Summarize each section in your own words
- **Ask questions**: Turn headings into questions, then read to find the answers
- **Connect**: Note how this content relates to other things you've read or experienced

## The SQ3R Method

SQ3R is a classic reading comprehension system:

1. **Survey**: Scan the structure (headings, summaries, visuals)
2. **Question**: Turn each heading into a question
3. **Read**: Read actively to answer your questions
4. **Recite**: Summarize what you just read without looking at the text
5. **Review**: Go back over the material periodically

This method forces you to engage with the material multiple times, each time from a different angle.

## Build Your Vocabulary

Unknown words are comprehension roadblocks. When you encounter an unfamiliar word:

1. Try to infer the meaning from context
2. Look it up if you can't
3. Write it down with a simple definition
4. Try to use it in conversation or writing within 24 hours

Reading widely — across different genres and topics — naturally builds vocabulary over time.

## Control Your Reading Environment

Comprehension requires focus. Your environment matters:

- **Reduce distractions**: Put your phone away, close unnecessary tabs, find a quiet space
- **Use a pointer**: Your finger, a pen, or an index card under each line keeps your eyes moving forward and prevents skipping back
- **Take breaks**: Reading for more than 45–60 minutes without a break reduces comprehension. Use a timer to schedule breaks

## Practice Deliberately

Like any skill, reading comprehension improves with deliberate practice. Set aside 20–30 minutes daily for focused reading. After each session, write a one-paragraph summary of what you read. Over time, your brain becomes more efficient at extracting and retaining information.

## Discuss What You Read

The ultimate test of comprehension is the ability to explain something to someone else. After finishing a book or article, try to explain the main ideas to a friend or colleague. If you struggle, you've identified what you don't fully understand — go back and reread those sections.

Improving your reading comprehension is one of the highest-leverage investments you can make. Better reading leads to better thinking, better learning, and better decisions.
