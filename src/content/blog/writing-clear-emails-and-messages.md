In a world of instant messaging and overflowing inboxes, clear writing is a superpower. Whether you're emailing a colleague, messaging a client, or writing a Slack update, the way you communicate directly impacts how quickly and accurately your message is received.

## Start with the Subject Line

The subject line is your first — and sometimes only — chance to get attention. Make it specific and actionable:

- "Meeting rescheduled to Thursday 3 PM" (clear)
- "Update" (vague)
- "Approval needed: Q3 budget by Friday" (actionable)
- "Hello" (ignored)

A good subject line tells the reader exactly what to expect and whether action is required.

## Lead with the Most Important Information

Put the key message or request in the first sentence or two. Busy readers often skim the first paragraph and decide whether to read the rest. Don't bury your main point behind pleasantries or background context.

- "Could you review the attached proposal by Friday?" — Then add context.
- Not: "I hope this email finds you well. I've been working on the proposal and wanted to share it with you. There are a few sections I'd love your feedback on..." — The request is buried.

## Use Short Paragraphs and White Space

Large blocks of text are intimidating and hard to scan. Keep paragraphs to 2–4 sentences. Use line breaks between ideas. This makes your message visually approachable and easier to process quickly.

## One Topic Per Message

If you need to discuss multiple unrelated topics, send separate messages. This makes it easy for the recipient to forward, archive, or act on each item independently. A message about "project timeline, lunch order, and office搬家" is guaranteed to create confusion.

## Choose the Right Tone

Match your tone to your relationship and the context:

- **Formal**: first contact, external clients, official communications
- **Professional**: day-to-day work with colleagues
- **Casual**: close teammates, quick questions, familiar channels

When in doubt, err toward professional. You can always loosen up as the relationship develops.

## Be Specific About Actions

If you need someone to do something, make it crystal clear:

- "Please send me the report by end of day Wednesday."
- Not: "I'll need that report sometime soon."

Use bold or bullet points for action items. Include deadlines, deliverables, and any dependencies.

## Read Before You Send

A quick read-through catches typos, tone issues, and missing information. Read your message out loud — if it sounds awkward spoken, it will read awkwardly too. For important messages, wait 60 seconds before hitting send, then re-read with fresh eyes.

## Choose the Right Channel

Not every message needs to be an email. Use this framework:

- **Email**: formal requests, detailed information, external communication
- **Instant message**: quick questions, updates, casual coordination
- **Phone/video call**: complex discussions, sensitive topics, relationship building
- **Document**: detailed proposals, project plans, reference material

Choosing the right channel shows respect for everyone's time.

## End with Clarity

Close your message with a clear next step or summary. Even a simple "Let me know if you have questions" sets expectations. For action-oriented messages, restate what you need and by when.

Good writing is good thinking made visible. Practice these principles, and your messages will get clearer responses and faster results.
